Your answer:

Good. Now here are three guesses people usually make. For each one, decide Yes, that's it or No, that's not it before you reveal the answer — committing to a guess first is the point.

"It just made the picture smaller on the screen."

The computer shrank the photo down so it takes up fewer pixels — a smaller picture, so a smaller file.

Is that it?

Resizing a photo down genuinely does make the file smaller — but that isn't what happened here. Both versions are the same size on screen; that's the whole point of the comparison. Version B has just as many pixels as Version A. Something else got smaller, and it wasn't the dimensions.

"It zipped it up, like a ZIP folder."

The computer packed the file up tightly, the way a ZIP file squeezes documents together.

Is that it?

Close, and this is a real technique — it's called lossless compression, and ZIP is exactly that. It squeezes a file down by spotting repetition and storing it more efficiently, and you get every single bit of the original back when you unpack it. But lossless compression can't get you anywhere near twenty-three times smaller on a photo. To shrink a photo that hard, something has to actually be thrown away.

"It threw some of the information away on purpose."

The computer permanently deleted some of the detail in the photo, betting that you wouldn't notice.

Is that it?

Exactly. This is called lossy compression. The format's designers worked out which details human eyes are worst at noticing — subtle colour shifts, fine texture in busy areas — and threw those away. Not hidden, not compressed, not recoverable: gone. Open Version B and save it again and the discarded detail does not come back, because it isn't in the file any more. The gamble is that you can't tell, and at normal viewing size the gamble usually pays off.

Key idea: there are two different things people mean by "compressing a file." One keeps everything and just stores it more cleverly. The other permanently throws some of it away. Card 2 was the first kind, Card 3 was the second, and telling them apart is what Period 1 is about.

Look back at the answer you wrote at the top of this section. Did you describe throwing information away, or storing it more efficiently? Either is a reasonable first guess — most people don't know there are two different answers until someone tells them.

Section 2 · Two kinds

Two kinds of compression

Compression means shrinking a file so it's faster and cheaper to store or to send.

There are two kinds, and the difference between them is the single most important idea in this period.

What happensCan you get the original back exactly?Real examples
Lossless compressionShrinks the file by finding and removing repetition — no information is thrown awayYes — perfectly, bit for bitZIP, PNG images, FLAC audio
Lossy compressionShrinks the file by permanently discarding some information the format's designers judged you wouldn't noticeNo — some detail is gone for goodJPEG images, MP3/AAC audio, MP4/H.264 video

Say it to yourself this way: Lossless means you can get back the exact original, bit for bit. Lossy means some of the original is gone forever — you're trading a smaller file for less-than-perfect quality.

Neither one is "better." The right choice depends on what the data is for. Lossless is safe but doesn't shrink a file as much. Lossy shrinks a file far more, but you're gambling that nobody notices what's missing.

Why this matters for sending data

A smaller file needs less time and less data allowance to send. That's the whole reason video calls, music streaming and social media photo uploads all use lossy compression — without it, they'd be too slow and too expensive to send over an ordinary internet connection.

Here's the side-effect that gives it away. Download a friend's photo from Instagram, then re-upload it to your own post. It gets compressed again, on top of a round of loss that already happened. Do that a few times and the picture visibly falls apart. That's not a myth or a rumour — it's lossy compression stacking, and it's the clearest everyday proof that something really is being thrown away rather than just packed tighter.

Quick check: Which kind of compression lets you get the exact original file back?

Yes — lossless. Every bit of the original is recoverable, which is why ZIP is safe for documents and code where a single changed character would matter.

Not quite — that's the wrong way round. Lossy is the one that permanently throws information away. Lossless is the one you can fully recover. Re-read the two cards above; getting these two the wrong way round is the single most common mix-up in this topic.

Not quite — it's lossless only. No software can recover what a lossy format deleted, because the deleted detail is not stored anywhere in the file. There's nothing left to recover it from.

Not quite — it's lossless. Lossless compression genuinely loses nothing; it just stores the same information more efficiently, the way ZIP does. Only lossy compression discards anything.

Section 3 · How it actually works

How compression actually works

You now know there are two kinds. This section is about what each one actually does to the bits — and it's the part that makes the rest of this page make sense rather than just be a list to remember.

What the video covers (read this whether or not you watched it)

1. Lossless trick one — run-length encoding. Lots of files contain long runs of exactly the same thing: a stretch of white pixels across the top of a screenshot, a run of silence in a recording. Instead of storing white, white, white, white, white, you store 5 × white. Same information, far fewer bits, and you can rebuild the original exactly. That's why PNG is so good at screenshots and logos — they're full of flat areas — and comparatively poor at photos, which have almost no exact repeats.

2. Lossless trick two — a dictionary of the common bits. The second trick is to stop giving every symbol the same amount of space. Work out which patterns appear most often, give those the shortest codes, and give rare patterns longer ones. The file carries a small dictionary explaining the codes. Because common things now take fewer bits, the total shrinks — and again nothing is lost, because the dictionary lets you rebuild the original exactly. The classic version of this is called Huffman coding, and almost every lossless format uses some form of it, ZIP included.

3. Lossy — throwing away what you won't notice. Both tricks above have a hard limit. If a file has no repetition and no lopsided pattern frequencies, there is nothing left to squeeze. To get much smaller than that, you have to delete something. Lossy formats delete on purpose, guided by what human senses are worst at: JPEG throws away fine colour detail, because eyes notice changes in brightness far more than changes in colour. MP3 throws away sounds that louder sounds are drowning out at the same moment. This is called perceptual coding — compressing against the limits of human perception rather than against the data itself.

The one sentence that ties it together: lossless compression removes redundancy — things the file said twice. Lossy compression removes information — things the file only said once, that you probably won't miss.

Try it: run-length encoding

Reading about it isn't the same as doing it. Here's one row of pixels from a black-and-white image. W is a white pixel, B is a black one. Twenty pixels:

WWWWWWWBBBWWWWWWWWWW

Write it as run-length encoding: for each run, the number of repeats then the letter, in order, left to right. The first run is seven whites, so your answer starts 7W. No spaces, no commas.

What's actually in a terms of use policy

Before you read anything about them: have you ever actually read the terms before clicking "I agree"?

Almost nobody has. That's not a criticism — the average app's terms of use runs to thousands of words of legal English, and there's a button right there that makes it go away. But it does mean most people are agreeing to a real, legally binding contract without knowing what's in it.

A terms of use policy — or a EULA, an End User Licence Agreement — is a legal agreement between you and a software company. It sets out what they can do with your data and what you're allowed to do with their product. Clicking "I agree" is a genuine legal step, even though almost nobody reads what they're agreeing to. That gap — between what's actually written and what most people assume is written — is what this period is about reading past.

Almost every one of them is built from the same six kinds of clause. Once you know the six, you can skim any set of terms and find what matters in a couple of minutes.

Typical clauseWhat it covers
Data collectionWhat information the app collects about you — account details, location, usage, contacts and so on
Data use and sharingWhat the company can do with that data, and whether it's shared with or sold to other companies
Content ownershipWho owns something you create or upload — and what rights the company gets to use it
Acceptable useThe rules for what you're allowed to do with the product
LiabilityWhat the company is and isn't responsible for if something goes wrong
Dispute resolutionWhat happens if you and the company disagree — for example, whether you can join a class-action lawsuit or must go to individual arbitration

"Arbitration" is worth knowing. It means a disagreement gets settled privately, by a paid arbitrator, instead of in a public court. It sounds like a small procedural detail and it isn't: it affects whether large groups of affected users can act together against a company at all. It's one of the most consequential clauses in real terms of use, and one of the least noticed.

One person's click, everyone's pattern

Panel A — One user

Agrees to a data-collection clause without reading it. It feels like a small, private decision about one app on one phone. If it turns out badly, it's one person's problem.

Panel B — Millions of users

Agree to the same clause, the same way, without reading it. Now personal data about almost everyone is concentrated in the hands of a small number of companies. Nobody voted for that and nobody individually chose it — it's the sum of millions of identical small decisions.

That's the shift this period is asking you to make: from "what does this clause mean for me?" to "what does this clause mean when everybody agrees to it?" A clause can be perfectly legal, clearly written and freely agreed to, and still produce a result nobody would have chosen if they'd been asked directly. Legal and ethical are different questions.

A note before you go looking. Some of what you find in a real app's terms is going to surprise you, and some of it may bother you. That's a normal reaction and it means you're reading properly.

The point of this is not that everything you use is secretly awful. It's that you now know how to check before you agree to something — which is a genuinely useful skill that most adults don't have. If something you read worries you specifically, that's worth raising with your teacher rather than sitting with it.

Quick check: A clause says the company can share your data with third-party advertising partners. Which category is it?

Yes — Data use and sharing. Notice the difference from Data collection: collection is what they gather about you, use and sharing is what they then do with it and who else gets it. The two go together but they're separate clauses, and a company can be very open about one and very vague about the other.

Close, and this is the mix-up worth getting straight. Data collection is what information they gather about you in the first place. Data use and sharing is what happens to it afterwards — including passing it to other companies. This clause is about what happens afterwards, so it's Data use and sharing.

Not quite — content ownership is about things you make and upload: your photos, your posts, your videos. This clause is about data about you being passed to other companies, which is Data use and sharing.

Not quite — liability is about who's responsible when something goes wrong. This clause isn't about blame or fault; it's about your data being passed on, which is Data use and sharing.

Is it fair to call GIF "lossless"?

Back in Section 4, GIF went in the "it's complicated" bin. Here's the argument properly.

A GIF's compression step throws away no pixel data at all — decompress it and you get back exactly what was stored. But a GIF can hold only 256 colours at once, so saving a photograph as a GIF throws thousands of colours away before the compression starts, and it looks visibly worse.

Write your argument first — the models unlock once you've committed to your own.

Model A — arguing "lossless is fair":

I think calling GIF lossless is fair, because "lossless" describes what a compression algorithm does to the data it's given, and GIF's algorithm discards nothing — decompress a GIF and every stored pixel comes back exactly. The colour loss people point to is real, but it happens in a separate step before compression, when the image is reduced to 256 colours. That's a limitation of the format's colour depth, not of its compression. If we let the colour reduction change the label, we'd have to call almost every format lossy, because nearly all of them make some choice about how much data to keep before compressing.

Model B — arguing "it's misleading":

I think calling GIF lossless is misleading, because the label is supposed to tell a user whether they'll get their image back, and with GIF they won't. Saving a photo as a GIF permanently destroys thousands of colours, and no software can recover them — which is exactly what "lossy" is supposed to warn you about. Saying the compression step alone is lossless is technically true and practically useless: nobody chooses a format by which sub-step discards the data, they choose it by whether their picture survives. A label that's accurate about the algorithm and wrong about the outcome is a bad label.

Both models are good answers. They reach opposite conclusions, and both would be marked well, because the marking is on the reasoning and not on the side. That's genuinely how this question works.

Section 12 · Wrap-up

Wrap-up

Two periods, two ideas.

Compression is shrinking a file so it's faster and cheaper to store or send — and it comes in two kinds. Lossless finds what a file says twice and stores it once, so you can recover the exact original. Lossy permanently deletes information you probably won't miss, which shrinks a file far further. Almost everything you stream, upload or share is lossy, because without it the internet you actually use wouldn't be fast enough or cheap enough to run.

A terms of use policy or EULA is a real legal agreement built from six standard kinds of clause. Clicking "I agree" is a real legal step, and when millions of people take the same step the same way, individual clauses turn into societal patterns nobody chose. You can now open a real one, skim it, and find what matters — which is more than most adults can say.
